\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Whether model confidence can triage qualitative coding turns out to depend less on which model is used than on three choices
a researcher makes: how the confidence is judged, how the hand-coded sample is drawn, and how automation is counted. We take
each in turn, add what the results say about the human labels themselves, and then place the findings in prior work and
draw out what they mean for researchers and for the builders of decision models.

\paragraph{Judge confidence by its ranking, on your own data.} Calibration error was the wrong yardstick throughout. It is
cheap to fix after the fact (\S\ref{sec:eq1}), and it says little about what triage needs, which is that a coder's mistakes
receive lower confidence than its correct labels. That ranking varied widely within the decision-model category, from worse
than a cheap LLM's verbalized confidence to close to a frontier LLM's (\S\ref{sec:rq1}), and it varied across tasks for the
same coder. Two mechanisms appear to be at work. Large ties at the top of the scale, which no threshold can split, limit
some coders (\S\ref{sec:eq3}). And the read-out may matter: two decision models rank errors clearly better than the
verbalized confidence of a related instruct model, though only slightly better than its token probabilities, which is
consistent with the hypothesis that reading out a probability is much of the advantage (\S\ref{sec:eq4}). Per-code recalibration narrows
most of the remaining differences, partly by learning which codes are hard. None of this can be read off a product
description; it has to be measured on the researcher's own codebook.

\paragraph{A guarantee is a property of the sampling design.} The usual practice, tuning a threshold to 5\% error on
hand-coded data, misses its target about half the time, and a standard guarantee that treats decisions as independent fails
because decisions within a comment or a transcript err together (\S\ref{sec:eq2}). What made a guarantee valid was not a
better model but a better account of how the hand-coded sample was drawn: our corpus rule needs only that the hand-coded
units are a random sample of the corpus. The same account explains its cost. Whole transcripts of very unequal length carry
little evidence each, so on dialogue nothing could be certified at our budgets. Sampling single utterances removed that
obstacle, although the coders' confident dialogue decisions then gave too little evidence. Hand-coding for a guarantee is therefore a sampling-design decision, of the kind survey methodology has long
studied \citep{horvitz1952generalization}, and it should be planned before coding starts.

\paragraph{Count reading saved and codes found, not decisions accepted.} In a sparse multi-label codebook most decisions are
easy calls that a code does not apply. A coder can accept most of them, and a baseline that reads no text accepts 75\% at low
error, while finding few of the codes that apply and leaving most comments for a person to read (\S\ref{sec:rq1},
\S\ref{sec:eq5}). Accepted share is a convenient number, but the quantities a researcher feels are the codes found and the
segments still read. On those, the differences between coders are larger than on accepted share, and a more accurate,
costlier coder can save more work than a cheaper one.

\paragraph{Low confidence partly marks contested items.} Model uncertainty was higher where human coders disagreed
(\S\ref{sec:rq2}), no coder fell below the range of individual annotators, and adjudication often sided with the model on
sampled disagreements (\S\ref{sec:eq6}). Error measured against a single majority label may therefore overstate some model
errors, and a 5\% target against such a reference is partly a target against annotator noise
\citep{plank2022problem,uma2021learning}. Triage then has a useful side effect: the items it sends to a person include many
on which human judgement is genuinely needed. Agreement with annotators, which we measure, is still not the same as
interpretive validity, which we do not.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.} Where our design overlaps with the concurrent evaluations of decision models
\citep{ibrahim2026decision,sharma2026typedbench}, the findings agree: Jev trails the strongest LLMs in accuracy, its raw
calibration beats most LLM signals but not the frontier models, and cascades save cost. Our results add a caution to
coverage reported at fixed confidence thresholds, there and in confidence-based routing of qualitative codes
\citep{zhao2025confidence}: such thresholds need to be checked on held-out data from the same corpus, or certified, because
they overshoot and do not transfer. That they do not transfer matches \citet{lu2026candidate}; the read-out hypothesis is
consistent with \citet{li2026llmasjev}; and the evidence that majority labels contain errors echoes \citet{liu2026agreement}.
Guarantees for annotation already exist \citep{zeng2026hypac}; our contribution there is to show that, for coding data, which
guarantee is valid depends on how the hand-coded sample is drawn.

\paragraph{A recipe for model-assisted coding.} For a researcher who wants to code a corpus with a fixed codebook and check
only part of it by hand, the results suggest four steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Hand-code a random sample of units from the corpus itself, and set part of it aside for certification. Units can be
  comments, transcripts, random fixed-size blocks of turns, or single utterances coded with their context; units of similar
  weight make a guarantee far cheaper (\S\ref{sec:eq2}). Random selection is what makes the guarantee possible; thresholds
  from other codebooks, and fixed rules such as ``accept $\ge$0.95'', did not carry over in our data.
  \item On the other part, a pilot sample, choose the coder and its scores. Compare how many decisions each candidate
  accepts at the target, which after recalibration depends mostly on accuracy; low ECE is not a sufficient criterion
  (\S\ref{sec:eq1}). Fit any per-code maps here: they improved error ranking for every coder with enough calibration data,
  but maps fitted on the certification sample would void its guarantee (\S\ref{sec:eq3}). Compare the accepted share with a
  prevalence baseline that reads no text, and check how many applying codes the accepted decisions contain (\S\ref{sec:rq1}).
  \item Freeze the chosen coder, scores and threshold grid, then certify on the set-aside sample with a rule that respects how
  it was drawn, such as corpus LTT. Each certificate covers one configuration: choosing among configurations by their
  certification results needs all of them in one testing family. Our version needed about 120 error-free units of similar
  weight before it certified anything; with whole transcripts of very unequal length it could not certify even a perfect
  coder (\S\ref{sec:eq2}).
  \item Count what a person still has to read, including the hand-coded sample: in multi-label coding, a segment leaves the
  queue only when all its codes are accepted (\S\ref{sec:eq5}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{For builders of decision models.} Decision models are marketed as calibrated, and most of the seven are close to
it, or can cheaply be made so. The results point to what would make them more useful for coding: reporting error ranking and
risk--coverage on human-coded data, not only calibration; resolving the top of the confidence scale instead of placing many
decisions at exactly 1.0 (\S\ref{sec:eq3}); making outputs invariant to option order, or averaging over orders, since order
changed up to 55\% of one model's labels in a single task (\S\ref{sec:rq3}); and documenting which outputs are deterministic
(\S\ref{sec:eq6}).

\paragraph{From labels to estimates.} Triage bounds the error rate among the labels a researcher accepts, in aggregate; it
says nothing about any one label. Many qualitative studies go on to report code frequencies or associations between codes.
Prediction-powered and design-based estimators correct such quantities using a hand-coded subsample, without per-item
guarantees \citep{angelopoulos2023ppi,egami2023using}, and model confidence can decide which items to hand-code
\citep{gligoric2025unconfident}. The calibration sample that triage needs can serve these estimators too, and they do not
need a certified threshold, which makes them attractive on dialogue data, where we could certify almost nothing under either
sampling design.

\section{Limitations and follow-ups}
\label{sec:limitations}
Each limitation below comes with what would address it.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scope.} Five tasks on short segments (comments, utterances, sentences) from three public datasets, and pooled
  estimates describe these tasks, not a population of codebooks. We have no long-form interviews: public interview data with
  applied codes is rare, and a private dataset requires ethics approval. A coded interview corpus, used with consent for
  third-party processing, would test whether the results carry over to the setting where qualitative coding is most common.
  \item \textbf{A dated snapshot.} Several APIs were in public beta, and the models will change. We release the harness, the
  analysis plan and every coding decision, so every number can be recomputed from the public datasets and the study re-run
  on newer models; the raw response text is withheld because it quotes the datasets.
  \item \textbf{Guarantees.} Corpus LTT assumes that the hand-coded clusters are a uniformly random subset of the corpus and
  that the test visits them in random orders; our split assigns clusters by a hash of their id, which we treat as random, and
  the orders come from a recorded seed. Each certificate covers one configuration. The procedure is conservative (in
  simulation it accepted less than the best safe threshold), and much of its cost on dialogue comes from scaling every
  transcript by the largest one. Sampling single utterances avoids most of that, although at our budget the coders' confident
  dialogue decisions still gave too little evidence. Procedures that handle unequal unit weights more efficiently, or larger
  hand-coded samples of small units, could certify more. The population version additionally needs independent clusters and
  a true bound on cluster weight; for it, random re-splits are only a diagnostic, while for the corpus guarantee they are a
  direct check.
  \item \textbf{Post-hoc analysis.} The plan was kept in the project repository, not externally registered. Sonnet was extended
  to the full sample after subset results were seen, the A3 analyses were revised after their first output, and the corpus
  guarantee, the prevalence analysis and several controls were added or revised after external reviews. All are labelled
  exploratory; a registered replication on new codebooks would confirm or correct them.
  \item \textbf{LLM read-out.} Token probabilities were read after a generated rationale, and multi-label verbalized
  probabilities were converted under a reading the prompt did not fix (\S\ref{sec:setup}); both may disadvantage the LLM
  baselines. We tested one alternative prompt; reading option probabilities directly, without a rationale, would test the
  read-out hypothesis of \S\ref{sec:eq4}.
  \item \textbf{Adjudication} covered a stratified, high-confidence-enriched sample of 200 items drawn from disagreements with
  six label sources. It relied partly on an LLM panel whose families overlap with the coders it judged (Claude Opus with Haiku
  and Sonnet; GPT-6 Sol with Luna and OpenAI Decisions), and the author, the only human adjudicator, did not repeat a sitting to
  measure their own consistency. Independent expert adjudicators would make the label-noise estimate firmer.
  \item \textbf{Measurement.} RQ2 rests on four AnnoMI transcripts and is unweighted. ECE is top-label calibration against the
  majority label. Costs are October 2026 list prices for inference only, Kev's are estimated warm-serving costs, and human
  reading is counted in segments, not timed; a timing study would turn the reading counts into hours.
  \item \textbf{Coverage gaps.} Kev-9B's robustness arms cover one to four tasks, and Kev-27B was run on the main and format
  arms only. Decision models return no text and could not be probed for memorization; Kev's published training data excludes
  our datasets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Model confidence can triage qualitative coding, but its value is easy to overstate. What matters is how well a coder's
confidence ranks its own errors, which calibration error does not reveal and which has to be measured on the researcher's own
codebook. An error target holds only under a rule that follows how the hand-coded sample was drawn: with one, models certified
most decisions on Reddit comments, almost all of them calls that a code does not apply, and almost none on dialogue at our
budgets. Decision models make confidence cheap to obtain, and the best configurations came within two points of a frontier LLM's
certified automation on comments at a fraction of its cost. Whether that automation saves real work depends on what is left
for a person to read.

\section*{Ethics statement}
All data are public and were collected and annotated by their original authors; we used them only for evaluation. Only these
public texts were sent to commercial APIs; no private or newly collected data were used. AnnoMI's counselling conversations
are transcribed from publicly available demonstration videos of motivational interviewing. The only human participant was
the author, as adjudicator.
