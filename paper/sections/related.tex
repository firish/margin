\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLMs for qualitative and content coding.} LLMs have been used to apply codebooks deductively
\citep{xiao2023supporting,chew2023llm}, to replace or assist crowd annotation \citep{gilardi2023chatgpt,ziems2024can}, to
code open-ended survey responses \citep{mellon2024ais} and to score classroom discourse \citep{wang2023chatgpt}, and
\citet{dunivin2025scaling} showed that chain-of-thought prompting can match human coders on some hermeneutic codes. Most of
this work asks how well a model agrees with human coders, and best-practice guidance accordingly stresses validation against
human labels \citep{pangakis2023automated,tornberg2024best}. Recent studies refine the workflow: they compare prompting
strategies \citep{rodrigueztriana2026one}, benchmark many models against expert adjudication \citep{marston2026humanitarian},
design human--AI workflows for deductive coding \citep{li2025fallshort,matveyenko2025blended,yuan2026calico} and add model
self-critique \citep{dunivin2026selfreflection}. Fewer ask which of a model's labels can be trusted. \citet{zhao2025confidence}
come closest: they combine self-confidence with inter-model diversity to route segments to review, auto-accepting 35\% at
under 5\% error on their data. We ask whether such thresholds hold on held-out data and across codebooks, and what it takes
to guarantee them. Agreement with human labels is itself an imperfect yardstick: \citet{liu2026agreement} show that blind
experts often prefer the model's code to the human consensus, and our adjudication points the same way (\S\ref{sec:eq6}).

\paragraph{Decision models.} Jev introduced the ``System One'' interface \citep{typesafe2026jev}. \citet{denney2026jev}
compare Jev with LLMs on seven political-science replications for accuracy, cost and calibration. \citet{li2026jevjudge}
use it as an accept-or-escalate judge, and \citet{rafe2026crash} convert crash narratives into probabilistic variables.
\citet{ibrahim2026decision} and TypedBench \citep{sharma2026typedbench} are closest to our work (\S\ref{sec:intro}). Other
recent studies show that short, natural-sounding context can flip decision models' answers \citep{xu2026jevout}, that
rejection thresholds fitted on one dataset fail on another \citep{lu2026candidate}, and that decisions can be read from an
ordinary LLM's next-token probabilities without retraining, with option-order bias reduced by averaging over rotations
\citep{li2026llmasjev,zhang2026anyjev}. Relative to this work, we add the newer hosted and open models
\citep{openai2026decisions,cloudflare2026clef,perplexity2026decider,palmer2026kev}, real qualitative codebooks with human
disagreement, a test of whether thresholds keep their error targets, guarantees that respect how the hand-coded sample was
drawn, and a comparison of related 9B systems.

\paragraph{Confidence and calibration.} Neural classifiers are often miscalibrated, and temperature scaling
\citep{guo2017calibration}, Platt scaling \citep{platt1999probabilistic} and isotonic regression
\citep{zadrozny2002transforming} are standard fixes. Whether confidence separates a model's correct from incorrect
predictions is a separate question \citep{hendrycks2017baseline}, and it is the one triage depends on. For LLMs, confidence
can be read from token probabilities \citep{kadavath2022language} or elicited verbally \citep{tian2023just,xiong2024can}, and
fast black-box estimators are being developed \citep{hayes2026pinocchio}. We compare these signals with decision models'
probabilities and keep calibration and error ranking apart throughout (\S\ref{sec:eq1}).

\paragraph{Selective prediction and error guarantees.} Selective classification accepts a prediction only above a
confidence threshold \citep{geifman2017selective}, and cascades send the rest to a stronger model \citep{chen2024frugalgpt}.
Two kinds of guarantee exist for such thresholds. Conformal risk control \citep{angelopoulos2024crc} bounds an expected
monotone loss under exchangeability; standard Learn-then-Test \citep{angelopoulos2025ltt} gives a high-probability bound on
population risk from valid tests, under assumptions such as i.i.d.\ calibration data. When observations come in groups, the
group becomes the unit of analysis \citep{dunn2023hierarchical}, and for a fixed corpus a finite-population betting test
\citep{waudbysmith2024betting} needs only that the hand-coded units, and the order they are visited in, are random. These
guarantees have been applied to LLM judges \citep{jung2025trust,badshah2026judge,li2026localize}, hybrid LLM--human annotation
\citep{zeng2026hypac}, human verification of LLM labels \citep{wang2024human} and calibrated grading
\citep{raikote2026chillgrader}. We adapt the finite-population test to coding decisions, averaging over visiting orders with
e-values \citep{vovk2021evalues}, and measure three things on real codebooks: how often the usual rule without a guarantee
breaks its target, whether a guarantee that ignores clustering fails, and how much coverage a valid one costs at calibration
sizes a research team can realistically hand-code.

\paragraph{Human label variation and downstream inference.} Annotators disagree for principled reasons, so a single
majority label is an imperfect target \citep{plank2022problem,uma2021learning}. \citet{baan2022stop} argue that
calibration should then be judged against the distribution of human labels, and \citet{lee2023dissenting} test whether LLMs
reproduce that distribution. Our RQ2 asks the triage version of this question: whether model uncertainty is high where
humans split. A complementary line of work does not accept individual labels at all. It corrects downstream estimates,
such as code prevalences or regression coefficients, using a hand-coded subsample
\citep{angelopoulos2023ppi,egami2023using}, and can use model confidence to choose which items to hand-code
\citep{gligoric2025unconfident}.

\paragraph{Contamination.} Public benchmarks may be in training data. We adapt the guided-versus-general completion test of
\citet{golchin2024time} and add a decoy-name control to separate memory from task framing.
