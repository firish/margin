\section{Results: research questions}

\subsection{RQ1: Does confidence triage qualitative coding?}
\label{sec:rq1}

Table~\ref{tab:main_rq1} summarizes agreement and ranking for every signal, Table~\ref{tab:main_claims} gives the
confirmatory contrasts, and Figures~\ref{fig:forest} and~\ref{fig:rc} show the contrasts and the per-task risk--coverage
curves.

\paragraph{Raw error ranking varies widely among decision models.} The open Kev-27B ranks its own errors best among the
decision models (error AUROC 0.788), close to Sonnet's verbalized confidence (0.801). pplx-decider (0.764) and Jev (0.756)
follow; OpenAI Decisions, Clef, Clef-flash and Kev-9B rank errors worse (0.694--0.736). In the pre-specified contrasts, Jev
ranks its errors better than four of the six other decision models: OpenAI Decisions by \res{rq1/jev/oaid/auroc/dci}, Clef by
\res{rq1/jev/clef/auroc/d}, Clef-flash by \res{rq1/jev/clef_flash/auroc/d} and Kev-9B by \res{rq1/jev/kev9b/auroc/d}. Its
difference from pplx-decider is not significant (\res{rq1/jev/pplx/auroc/dci}); pplx-decider accepts more decisions under the
naive 5\%-target rule (\res{rq1/jev/pplx/cov/dci}). Kev-27B beats Jev on both primary metrics (\res{rq1/jev/kev27b/auroc/d}
AUROC; \res{rq1/jev/kev27b/cov/d} coverage). Among LLM signals, Jev beats every token-probability signal, by 0.20 AUROC for
Luna and gpt-oss and by 0.07 for the Qwen models, as well as 3-LLM agreement (\res{rq1/jev/agree3/auroc/d}), self-consistency
(\res{rq1/jev/luna_sc-vote/auroc/d}) and gpt-oss's verbalized confidence (\res{rq1/jev/oss-verbal/auroc/d}). The verbalized
confidence of Haiku (\res{rq1/jev/haiku-verbal/auroc/d}) and Sonnet beats it: \res{rq1/jev/sonnet_sub-verbal/auroc/dci} on the
pre-specified Sonnet subset ($p=\res{rq1/jev/sonnet_sub-verbal/auroc/p}$) and \res{rq1/jev/sonnet-verbal/auroc/dci} on the
full-sample extension. Jev's advantage over Luna's verbalized confidence is borderline (\res{rq1/jev/luna-verbal/auroc/dci},
bootstrap $p=\res{rq1/jev/luna-verbal/auroc/p}$) and not a claim after Holm adjustment, and the exploratory Qwen3.8-2.4T's
verbalized confidence edges out Jev (\res{rq1/jev/qwen_big-verbal/auroc/dci}). Token
probabilities rank errors poorly because they saturate: 96--100\% of decisions carry confidence $\ge$0.99.

\paragraph{How much can be accepted, and what is it?} Under the naive rule, Kev-27B, Sonnet and pplx-decider accept 41--44\% of
all decisions and Jev 33\% (Table~\ref{tab:main_policies}; equal-weight means over the five tasks), but 6.2--7.5\% of what they
accept is wrong. Acceptance is far higher on MFRC, where these four accept 78--94\% of decisions, than on the four dialogue
tasks (22--32\% on average). Most MFRC decisions are easy negatives: 89\% of MFRC test decisions are codes that do not apply. A
prevalence baseline that reads no text, predicting ``absent'' for every code with confidence equal to that code's
calibration share of absent labels, accepts 75\% of MFRC decisions at 3.6\% error under the naive rule
(Table~\ref{tab:main_mfrc}), although it carries no certificate (\S\ref{sec:eq5}). The models differ in what the baseline
cannot do: find the codes that apply. Among accepted
decisions, pplx-decider labels 62\% of the applying codes correctly, Sonnet 53\% and Jev 35\%; the rest go to a human or are
missed. On the four dialogue tasks, accepted shares are much smaller, and the naive rule's error targets mostly fail
(\S\ref{sec:eq2}).

\paragraph{Robustness.} Against the adjudicated reference, 39 of the 40 Jev contrasts (20 comparators, two metrics) keep their
sign and significance. The exception is the borderline error-ranking contrast with Luna's verbalized confidence, whose interval
moves from just above zero to just below it (\res{rq1_adj_plan_v1/jev/luna-verbal/auroc/dci}).

\subsection{RQ2: Does uncertainty sit where humans disagree?}
\label{sec:rq2}

When annotators disagree, a single majority label is a weak target \citep{plank2022problem,baan2022stop}, so we ask whether
model uncertainty is high where humans split. The evidence is limited: MFRC has 1,382 test comments with three annotators,
but AnnoMI's multi-annotated test items (91 client and 93 therapist utterances) come from only four transcripts, so the
AnnoMI results describe those conversations. With that caveat, model uncertainty rises where humans split
(Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}). For every decision model and the Luna, Haiku, gpt-oss and Sonnet verbal signals, the Spearman
correlation between model uncertainty and the entropy of the human labels is 0.24--0.43 on MFRC and 0.33--0.65 on AnnoMI,
except Kev-9B on AnnoMI client (0.18). Token probabilities, vote-based agreement and Qwen3.5-9B's verbalized confidence track
disagreement weakly or not at all; Luna's token-probability uncertainty is unrelated or inversely related to it ($-$0.10 and
$-$0.03 on AnnoMI). Coders run on a subset (Qwen3.8-2.4T, self-consistency) have only 20--23 multi-annotated AnnoMI items and
are not interpreted.

In a leave-one-annotator-out comparison, each annotator in turn is scored against the majority of the other annotators, and
each coder is scored against the same references (the dot is its mean over references). No coder falls below the range of
individual annotators, and some fall above it: on AnnoMI therapist, Jev's mean $\kappa$ (0.891) exceeds all ten annotators'
(0.631--0.886), and on MFRC Sonnet exceeds five of six. This is a descriptive comparison with a broad human range, not a test
of equivalence to a typical annotator.

\subsection{RQ3: How stable are decisions?}
\label{sec:rq3}

We perturbed the request on the test split (Figure~\ref{fig:rq3}). Jev, OpenAI Decisions, Clef-flash and pplx-decider ran
every arm on all applicable tasks; Kev-9B ran one to four tasks per arm, because its hosting stopped; Luna and Sonnet ran the
option-order and label arms on 85 test segments per task. Flip rates and changes in AURC, the two pre-specified outcomes,
are design-weighted, with per-task cluster-bootstrap intervals (Table~\ref{tab:tab06_rq3}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Option order matters, differently by model.} Averaged over tasks, reversing or shuffling options changes 4\%
  of Jev's labels, 5--7\% of pplx-decider's and Sonnet's, 8--11\% of OpenAI Decisions' and Luna's, under 1\% of Clef-flash's,
  and 13--31\% of Kev-9B's; in single tasks it reaches 55\% (Kev-9B, reversed options, TalkMoves teacher). For coders whose identical repeated requests already vary (2.1\% of Jev's labels, 3.3\% of Sonnet's, 6.6\% of Luna's;
  \S\ref{sec:eq6}), some order flips may reflect that variation; the samples differ, so the rates cannot simply be
  subtracted.
  \item \textbf{Wording matters a little.} Paraphrased definitions change 3--10\% of labels, and replacing code names with
  neutral identifiers 5--10\%. Asking for MFRC codes one at a time instead of together changes at most 4\% (Clef-flash);
  OpenAI Decisions' multi-label answers are exactly invariant to question order and batching.
  \item \textbf{Removing the previous turn removes information.} It changes 15--24\% of dialogue labels on average over tasks
  (up to 39\% in one task) and makes the coders
  worse: $\kappa$ falls by 0.012--0.041 and AURC rises by 0.010--0.037. These flips are mostly a loss of relevant context, not
  instability, in line with the finding that added context can redirect decision models \citep{xu2026jevout}.
  \item \textbf{Question format has model-specific effects.} Asking each exclusive option as a separate yes/no question
  improves error ranking for Clef-flash ($+$0.029) and pplx-decider ($+$0.022), hurts it for Kev-27B ($-$0.028), and produces no
  clear pooled change for Jev or OpenAI Decisions.
\end{itemize}
Changes in AURC under surface perturbations stay within $\pm$0.08 per task for every coder except Kev-9B, whose option-order
sensitivity raises AURC by up to 0.23. Jev's flips concentrate on its low-confidence decisions (AUROC 0.82--0.99), so its
instability mostly shows up as uncertainty.
