\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deductive qualitative coding applies a fixed codebook to text: each segment of an interview, dialogue or survey answer is
read, compared with the code definitions and labelled, often twice to establish reliability \citep{krippendorff2019content}.
Large language models (LLMs) can now apply codebooks with useful accuracy
\citep{xiao2023supporting,chew2023llm,dunivin2024scalable,ziems2024can,gilardi2023chatgpt,matveyenko2025blended}, but
accuracy alone does not tell a researcher which of a model's labels to trust. The practical promise is \emph{triage}: accept
the labels the model is confident about, and send the rest to a person.

Consider a researcher with 1,500 counselling utterances to code and time to check 400 by hand. A model labels every
utterance with a confidence, and a threshold decides which labels to accept. Whether this works depends on three properties
of the confidence that are easy to conflate. \emph{Error ranking}: do the model's mistakes receive lower confidence than its
correct labels? \emph{Calibration}: does a stated 90\% mean 90\% correct? \emph{Selective risk and coverage}: at the chosen
threshold, how many labels are accepted, and how many of those are wrong? The three can come apart. A well-calibrated model
can rank its errors poorly, and a threshold chosen on the 400 hand-coded utterances to give 5\% error can exceed 5\% on the
other 1,100. We ask whether model confidence can select low-error decisions reliably enough for this workflow.

A new class of products is marketed for exactly this purpose. We call them \emph{decision models}: interfaces that take
typed questions (yes/no, multiple choice) with criteria and return a probability for each option, without generating text.
TypeSafe's Jev \citep{typesafe2026jev} was followed within weeks by OpenAI's Decisions API \citep{openai2026decisions},
Cloudflare's Clef \citep{cloudflare2026clef}, Perplexity's pplx-decider \citep{perplexity2026decider} and the open Kev
models \citep{palmer2026kev}. The interface is new, but the idea behind it, probabilistic classification with the option to
abstain, is not \citep{geifman2017selective}. Whether these probabilities support triage on real codebooks, with human
disagreement, dialogue context and rare codes, is an open question. We study it for the classification step of deductive
coding on short segments (comments, utterances, sentences); inductive coding and long-form interviews are outside our scope.

We compare seven decision models with twelve LLM confidence signals (verbalized confidence, token probabilities and
agreement-based signals) on five human-coded tasks: moral foundations in Reddit comments, client and therapist talk in
counselling dialogues, and teacher and student moves in K-12 mathematics lessons. A prospectively specified analysis plan
fixes three research questions, each answered with pre-specified contrasts:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (triage).} Do decision models' probabilities rank their own coding errors better than LLM confidence
  signals, and what share of decisions can be accepted at $\le$5\% error?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (disagreement).} Is model uncertainty high where human coders disagree, and how does a model's agreement
  with annotators compare with annotators' agreement with each other?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (robustness).} How much do decisions change when the codebook or request is reworded or reordered, or
  when context is removed?
\end{itemize}
The research questions say what happens; six experimental questions ask why, and what it means in practice. Three examine
the answer to RQ1: whether low calibration error predicts useful triage (\textbf{EQ1}), whether threshold rules keep their
error target and what a finite-sample guarantee costs (\textbf{EQ2}), and whether triage is limited by poor ranking or by
large ties at the top of the confidence scale (\textbf{EQ3}). \textbf{EQ4} tests one explanation for decision models'
advantage, by comparing two 9B decision models with the confidence of a 9B instruct model from the same family.
\textbf{EQ5} turns the results into a practical choice: which configurations give the most guaranteed automation for their
cost, and how much reading they actually save. \textbf{EQ6} checks whether the results are artefacts of nondeterminism,
prompt choice, memorization or label noise.

The short answer to our title is: on Reddit comments, yes, with caveats; on dialogue, not at the budgets we studied. Two
facts drive both halves. Coding decisions come in clusters, such as the eight codes of one comment or the utterances of one
transcript, so an error guarantee has to follow how the hand-coded sample was drawn. And most decisions in a multi-label
codebook are easy calls that a code does not apply, so a high accepted share can mean little.

\paragraph{Relation to concurrent work.} Two studies released in the weeks before ours also evaluate decision models:
\citet{ibrahim2026decision} compare Jev and small open decision models with 19 LLMs on 15 computational-social-science
labelling tasks (accuracy, calibration, coverage at fixed thresholds, cost and cascades), and TypedBench
\citep{sharma2026typedbench} uses synthetic, rule-labelled tasks. Where the designs overlap, our findings agree: Jev trails
the strongest LLMs in accuracy, its raw calibration beats most LLM signals but not the frontier models, and cascades save
cost. Risk-controlled triage is not new either: HyPAC \citep{zeng2026hypac} routes items between models and humans with a
PAC guarantee on annotation error, and \citet{zhao2025confidence} triage qualitative codes by confidence and inter-model
diversity. Our contribution is an evaluation, not a new method, and it has four parts.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A multi-vendor evaluation} (all questions; \S\ref{sec:setup}). Seven decision models and twelve LLM
  confidence signals on five human-coded tasks, under a prospectively specified plan with dated amendments and a deviations
  log, with every coding decision released.
  \item \textbf{Ranking, not calibration, separates coders} (RQ1, EQ1, EQ3, EQ4; \S\ref{sec:rq1}, \S\ref{sec:eq1}--\S\ref{sec:eq4}).
  Decision models range from ranking their errors worse than a cheap LLM's verbalized confidence to nearly as well as a
  frontier LLM's. Calibration error does not predict where a model falls: task-level recalibration improves calibration while
  leaving the ranking of decision models and verbalized confidence unchanged, and per-code recalibration narrows most of the
  differences. A comparison of related 9B systems
  suggests that much of a decision model's advantage comes from reading out a probability rather than from its training.
  \item \textbf{Guarantees that respect the sampling design} (RQ1, EQ2; \S\ref{sec:rules}, \S\ref{sec:eq2}). Tuning a
  threshold to 5\% error on hand-coded comments overshoots in about half of re-splits, and a guarantee that treats decisions as
  independent fails because decisions within a comment or transcript err together. A corpus-level rule, adapted from betting
  tests for sampling without replacement, holds: after 30\% of comments are hand-coded, it certifies 60--83\% of the remaining
  decisions for most coders. On dialogue, with whole transcripts hand-coded, it certifies nothing. We show why: with whole transcripts as sampling units, our
  procedure could not certify even a perfect coder at our calibration sizes, and sampling single utterances removes that
  obstacle but leaves too little evidence of $\le$5\% error among the coders' confident decisions.
  \item \textbf{What automation means for coding} (EQ5; \S\ref{sec:eq5}, \S\ref{sec:discussion}). Accepted shares overstate
  the work saved. Nearly all certified comment decisions say a code does not apply, a baseline that reads no text accepts 75\%
  of comment decisions at 3.6\% error, and under every certified policy a person still reads most comments. Recall of applying
  codes, the share of segments still read and policy-specific costs give a fuller picture, and we turn them into a recipe for
  researchers.
\end{enumerate}
Beyond triage, model uncertainty is higher where human coders disagree (RQ2, \S\ref{sec:rq2}); option order and missing
context change some models' labels substantially (RQ3, \S\ref{sec:rq3}); and the checks of EQ6 bound the main threats: an
alternative prompt does not improve the LLM baselines, there is little evidence of memorization, and the contrasts barely
change against an adjudicated reference, although adjudication also suggests errors in some human labels
(\S\ref{sec:eq6}). Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the
design and the threshold rules, Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:eqs} answer the research and experimental questions,
and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what they mean for researchers.
